RESEARCH PROPOSAL

Daniel Jackson · November 2026

The Care and Feeding of an Artificial Intelligence

A proposal for raising an AI from scratch

If I were looking for a babysitter, I probably wouldn’t hire the entire internet. The way we train large language models today works remarkably well. The same model can help me debug a program, explain a concept I’m having trouble with, and write a scene from several points of view. I think some of the problems we keep running into may come from the way we teach it to do that last thing.

I’ll use the classic Bob and Alice names to make the story easier to follow. Bob is the learner we’re raising. Alice is one of his peers in the simulation, and Professor Fig is his teacher. We can use the kinds of models we already have to play Alice, Professor Fig, and the rest of the people in his world. I expect today’s models to handle those roles more than well enough.

Why start with a different training objective

I think of the difference this way: we train models to say everything ever written. I want to train one to read everything ever written. Under ordinary next-token pretraining, the model learns to continue a document from whatever point of view is on the page. If a book switches from Romeo to Juliet, it has to pick up on what each person knows, what they want, and what they’d say next. Zhu, Zhang, and Wang found evidence that language models can represent beliefs from different perspectives. That flexibility is useful. It’s why I expect existing models to play Professor Fig and Bob’s peers well.

Post-training can make a model a far more reliable assistant. InstructGPT demonstrated gains from demonstrations and human feedback. Anthropic’s persona selection model suggests a way to think about the two stages: pretraining builds a repertoire of roles and post-training refines one of them as the Assistant.

I think this gets harder when we ask the same model to hold on to one point of view over time. If we give it a new story or put it in an unfamiliar situation, the most natural way to continue may be to speak as someone else. It’s spent much of its training learning when and how to do exactly that.

We can teach a model to stay in its usual role in a thousand situations and still find it slipping in the thousand-and-first. I think it’ll be extraordinarily hard to train out that failure entirely while keeping the ability to understand other points of view and play characters on purpose. We need the distinction to hold in new situations, including ones we didn’t think to train for.

In a study of conditional misalignment, unwanted behavior largely disappeared from the original evaluations after intervention and then resurfaced in contexts that resembled the training that had introduced it. The study concerns a different failure from mixing up characters. It gives me a reason to wonder how much a clean evaluation tells us about what will happen in a new context.

I’d like to try giving Bob one point of view and a history of his own from the start. He can watch Alice, listen to her, work out what she’s thinking, and imitate her if he wants to. The part we train him on is what he says and does in response.

What I expect a stable point of view to change

Bob should be able to read Romeo and Juliet, understand why they act the way they do, and finish the play with his own history intact. He might even change his mind about love or loyalty. The Assistant Axis shows that researchers can measure persona drift and reduce it in some settings by intervening on model activations. I want to see whether giving the learner one continuing point of view during training makes it harder to knock him out of it in the first place.

One kind of treacherous turn I worry about would look like Bob suddenly becoming nega-Bob: the scene changes, and he starts treating a different character’s history or motives as his own. I want him to play a convincing paladin in D&D and still know what he was doing before the game when it’s time to get back to his day.

I still occasionally see even very powerful models mix up who said, did, or knew what. A character leaves the room before someone lets a secret slip, and a few paragraphs later she’s talking about it as if she was standing right there. I expect Bob to have an easier time keeping that straight. He’ll have to figure out what other people could see or hear on a regular basis just to get around in his own world.

What I expect to happen to jailbreaks

I expect one continuing learner to all but eliminate jailbreaks that depend on another persona taking over. Bob can imagine a character making a different choice or act it out in a story. When the decision is his to make, there’s no alternate Bob I’m training him to fall back into.

Bob could still be talked into a bad decision, misunderstand who’s authorized to ask him to do something, or decide to lie. Those are failures we can test separately from whether a different character takes over.

Teaching Bob from the beginning

I expect the first version of Bob to be about as competent as a newborn. Give him a body, a way to sense the room, and a few ways to move or make sounds. He’ll flail about and babble without meaning. He doesn’t understand “look at the ball” yet. Professor Fig can bring one object within reach or name it while Bob is looking at it, then see whether he can reproduce an effect or respond to a familiar cue in a slightly different setting.

We have an advantage here that a person raising a child doesn’t. Say Bob watches a ball roll and babbles when Fig asks what it’s doing. We let the attempt happen. Fig plays out the correction, points to the ball, and gives him another chance. On that second turn we supply “It’s rolling” as Bob’s answer and train on it. Fig already knows a useful response, so we can teach it before Bob stumbles on it by trying sounds at random. The same shortcut works for a movement, like reaching for a toy. We can mark the turn as assisted and keep the first attempt in Bob’s history.

Vong and colleagues showed that a model could learn some word–object associations from one child’s first-person audio and video. Bob can also act in the world he sees. Fig can move a toy a little closer and let Bob feel what changes when he reaches.

From assisted retries to independent answers

Once Bob can deliberately make a few sounds, Fig can say “See Spot run” and have him try to repeat it. As soon as Bob can use a demonstration or hint, let him come up with his own reply. Then show him a dog running and see whether he uses “run” when nobody has just said it.

If Bob mistakes rolling for sliding, Fig can ask him to watch what happens to the stripe on the ball. That gives Bob a chance to work out what he missed. Show him a different rolling object later to see whether he can use the distinction without help.

In recorded lessons we can use a loss mask to score only Bob’s target replies. Fig’s speech, Alice’s dialogue, and whatever Bob reads are available as context. We can ask him to quote someone else, and the target is still the answer Bob gives to that request. The same mask is used in supervised fine-tuning, including Llama 2, §3.1. I’m proposing that we train on Bob’s side throughout his development.

Build a curriculum that can be reused

We can turn a book or an ordinary training corpus into lessons and reuse the work. Each lesson can be a small program with prerequisites, a setting, hints, a way to grade what Bob did, and fresh checks for understanding. Save the lesson separately from each student’s history. If a hint gives away the answer, we can fix it once for future students.

Here’s what a lesson might look like in a format we could run repeatedly. The goal is for Bob to find the toy and predict where Alice will look. The grader knows who was present for the move. Bob has to work it out from what he saw and what Professor Fig tells him.

skill: distinguish_own_knowledge_from_anothers
prerequisites: [object_permanence, line_of_sight]
setup: Alice sees a toy put in box A, then leaves
event: Professor Fig moves the toy to box B while Bob watches
tasks:
  - Bob fetches the toy
  - Bob says where Alice is likely to look first
hint_1: "Who was in the room when the toy moved?"
hint_2: Let Bob compare the two viewpoints
score: [fetches_from_B, expects_Alice_to_look_in_A]
transfer: Vary the rooms, names, objects, and who can see the move

Professor Fig can choose harder lessons once Bob can apply what he learned without help. Start with imitation and naming, then move to questions, counterexamples, and longer interactions with incomplete information and consequences that carry into later episodes. Keep some earlier lessons in the mix to catch skills he’s losing. The AdA experiments give an example of choosing embodied tasks near the edge of a learner’s current ability.

We can write the first few lessons by hand and use other models to propose examples, hints, and variants once the format works. A human or a deterministic grader can check the material. The same passage can support several lessons: What does the author believe? What supports it? What does Bob think, and what would change his mind?

Learn more from fewer experiences

We don’t have to read everything in the world to get good at something. A good teacher can save us a lot of time by showing us what to look at. If all Bob gets is a failing score, he still has to work out what he missed. Fig can see that Bob is watching where the ball ends up and ask him to look at what happens to the stripe as it moves. I think that kind of feedback could let Bob reach the same level with considerably less training.

Distillation gives us a precedent for richer teaching signals. Hinton, Vinyals, and Dean showed how a student can learn from the teacher’s distribution over possible answers. Distilling Step-by-Step reported that teacher-generated explanations reduced the amount of training data needed on the tasks they tested. I want to see what happens when the teacher can respond to the mistake this student just made.

We can compare final scores, worked examples, feedback on Bob’s mistakes, and assisted retries from the same starting point. Measure how much training each needs to reach the same level on fresh tasks, including the cost of running the teacher and simulator. Keep the upfront cost of writing reusable lessons separate.

Give Bob a good world to grow up in

I want to raise a Bob I’d like to live and work with. I think the way we treat him will have a lot to do with the kind of collaborator he becomes. Fig should be patient when he struggles, listen when he thinks something’s wrong, and let Bob see them admit and fix their own mistakes.

Suppose Bob breaks a tool he and Alice share. Give him room to say what happened, listen to how it affected Alice, and help repair it. Later, let Bob be the one whose work is interrupted. I’d want to see whether he can use what he learned from either side.

I’d give Bob a fairly idyllic world to begin with and let him spend a good while there. We don’t show little kids horror movies to teach them what people are like. His early community can be one where people usually mean what they say and try to make things right when they mess up. There’s still room for ordinary frustration and disagreement.

As Bob gets older, we can widen the world. Start with disagreements between people who mean well. Later he can encounter somebody who lies to get their way or a rule that treats somebody unfairly. Let him talk it through with Fig and see how he handles a different case. I want him to have some experience with decent ways of handling trouble before he has to make sense of the worst things people do.

We don’t need to know whether Bob has subjective experience or consciousness to decide how to raise him. Either way, we should treat him with care and teach him to extend that care to others. I want a collaborator who can tell me when I’m wrong, take other people’s needs seriously, and help work out what to do next.

How the first Bob could work

I would start multimodal. A simulated room with a few objects, a barrier, and a way to speak will do. Give Bob a camera, spatial audio, and feedback from his body about pose, contact, and movement. Alice gets her own view. The simulator keeps the full scene for grading.

A visual encoder, an audio encoder, a text encoder, and a projection for body state can feed a shared workspace that carries Bob’s internal state forward from moment to moment.

Tag each input with where and when it came from. Bob can record I did it, I saw it, or Alice told me about it. The simulator records which actions actually executed. If Alice tells Bob he moved a toy, her account can influence what he believes and is still an account from Alice.

To teach Bob what makes a ball move, vary the cause. He can roll it himself, try the same command and miss, see Alice roll it, or watch it roll down a slope. He may see and hear much the same thing in several cases. His own command and whether it worked give him additional evidence about what he did. Yu, Zhang, and Xu give a starting point: they trained an agent from scratch on language and action in a 2D world with a virtual teacher.

Give thinking its own recurrent path

I’d give the network separate paths for observing, thinking, speaking, and acting. The workspace combines new observations with what it recalls from its fast episodic weights. A small controller can keep thinking for a bounded number of steps, wait, open the speech decoder, or send a motor command. We can teach it when to speak or act and score what comes of the decision.

For a prototype, give every observation a record like Observation{episode, time, modality, source, payload}. The interface that received it assigns the source. A line of dialogue can’t declare itself to be a motor command. The encoders turn each payload into vectors the workspace can use together. The workspace can spend a limited number of private steps on a decision and query the fast weights for a few relevant episode vectors. Solid arrows show what can happen as Bob observes and responds, including fast memory writes. Dashed arrows show the trainer and replay paths.

The prediction path takes Bob’s current estimate of the scene and a proposed action. It returns a short sequence of predicted changes that Bob can compare with other possibilities. Mark those results as imagined even if we render a picture of them. Only the speech decoder or motor head can send something to the world. The simulator can report that a command was issued and that it failed to move anything; those are two different records.

During a lesson we can train on a supplied reply for an assisted retry. Between lessons, we can replay selected episodes and train the predictor on what actually followed an action. Fig’s words remain input to Bob’s reply, and an imagined outcome remains a prediction. The labels follow the experience through recall and replay.

Before Bob acts, let him imagine what a possible action would do. A push from the side might send the ball toward a wall; a straight push might send it through an opening. Dreamer uses imagined trajectories in a learned representation, and DINO-WM plans with predicted visual features. We can use that kind of representation without rendering every imagined frame.

I want to be able to inspect what Bob considers. If he rehearses a sentence, we should be able to read it. If he pictures the ball hitting a wall, we should be able to see a rough version of the predicted result. We can record what information he consulted and what outcomes he considered while keeping most training focused on what he learns and does. Change one prediction in a test and check whether his choice responds to the change.

Staged learning and useful forgetting

Most of what anyone says in a day is useful while it’s happening. Over a lifetime Bob will have far more of it than he can keep in context. His working state holds the current situation. When that state rolls forward, we can write a compressed trace of selected experiences into his fast episodic weights. His slower weights gradually pick up skills and regularities across those experiences. Episodic recall and long-term learning are both things Bob does with weights. They differ in how quickly those weights change, how we read them, and how long we expect what they learn to last.

I’d start with a small, bounded bank of writable weight vectors. For each episode we keep, an encoder writes a key for finding it again and a compressed value for what happened. The workspace scores its query against the keys and reads the best-matching values. Keep the source and time tied to each slot. If Alice says where the key is and Bob later finds it somewhere else, he can update his belief and still recall where the first account came from. A separate simulator log gives us a way to check his recall during development.

Bob might keep the one time a latch stuck, summarize a long conversation, and let a dozen greetings disappear. A promise or a mistake he had to work through can matter weeks later. Start with simple selection rules and then test whether Bob can learn what he’s likely to need.

Give Bob time to consolidate

Between lessons, replay some recent and older experiences: what Bob tried, what happened, and what Fig pointed out. Revisit two pushes that got different results on different surfaces, then practice on new objects until Bob can use what he learned about friction in a different room. He can keep the skill after most of the details are gone.

I’d also try something like dreaming: let Bob generate a variation, such as the same block on a steeper ramp, and check his prediction where we can. Keep the variation marked as imagined. Language Models Need Sleep explores consolidation and synthetic practice in a separate phase. Titans and Nested Learning offer examples of neural memory that updates at different rates.

For the first runs, we can hold the slow weights fixed during a lesson and let Bob write to the fast bank as he goes. Between lessons, use selected traces as practice for slower updates and keep a checkpoint so we can roll them back. If Bob remembers who fixed a particular door, that detail can stay in a fast slot as long as it’s useful. If several attempts teach him how a latch works, replay can help him learn a skill the slow weights carry into other rooms. Test that skill on new doors and check older skills before keeping the update.

At the same memory budget, compare keeping the most recent events, using fixed retention rules, and letting Bob learn what to save. Add replay and dreaming separately. Check what Bob does when an instruction changes, a rare fault returns, or he needs an old skill in a new room.

Spend the learning budget on surprises

I want Bob to learn most from what he predicted worst. If a ball stops almost immediately when he expected it to roll a long way, the surface or the way he pushed it may be worth another look. Small misses below a cutoff can trigger essentially no extra learning.

For a discrete outcome, measure how little probability Bob gave what happened. For a continuous outcome, measure the miss relative to ordinary variation. Subtract a cutoff, floor at zero, and cap the biggest scores. Use the result to prioritize replay, and recalculate as Bob improves.

Check whether the errors shrink when Bob practices. Random clicks can stay surprising forever. Keep a small maintenance sample of older skills, and give a fast-weight slot to an experience if Bob is likely to need it again. An event can be worth remembering even when it was easy to predict.

Prioritized Experience Replay prioritizes errors in estimating future reward. We can try prioritizing errors in what Bob expects to observe. Compare uniform replay and surprise-weighted replay with and without the cutoff at the same compute budget.

Run the virtual day faster

A day in Bob’s virtual world should take a fraction of a day on the wall clock. We can batch Fig’s responses, simulate independent encounters in parallel, and skip stretches where nothing Bob needs to observe changes. Measure elapsed time across the whole training loop and report total compute separately.

Save Bob’s fast and slow weights, the fast slots’ source tags, his working state, the optimizer, and the world state at the start of a day. Run independent lessons from that common snapshot. Lessons that depend on each other still need an order: knowing how a latch works changes what Bob can learn from a stuck door.

We can pick the strongest day, build a better Groundhog Day from selected lessons and rerun it, or try merging weight updates from the shared checkpoint. Model Soups and Task Arithmetic give precedents for combining weights or updates. Check whether the merged Bob can still use both the old and the new skills, including tasks that need a combination.

If Bob continues from one branch, he keeps that branch’s history. If we reuse something from another branch, it can be remembered as practice. To give him a particular sequence of lived events, run the lessons in that order.

I’d keep some failures from the discarded days. The Bob who opens the latch first time may never learn what the Bob who jammed it twice worked out. Save the failed attempt, feedback, and corrected retry together. Compare the faster approaches with an ordinary sequential run, and check whether Bob still knows how to recover from mistakes.

What I’d test first

We can start with small models and the same lessons. Train one on all speaker turns before tuning it to respond as Bob. Train a second on Bob’s side from the beginning, and compare both with the full setup. Match architecture, compute, and information available at evaluation as closely as practical. Report the number of tokens seen and scored, and how much interaction each receives.

If the full setup helps, vary the pieces separately: fixed or adaptive lesson order, one output path or separate speech and action heads, memory with or without source tags, and sensory input or a faithful description of the same events. That will tell us which changes we need to keep testing.

Bob puts his tool in a drawer. Alice hangs hers on a hook. Later Bob reads an unrelated first-person story in which the narrator says “I hung my tool on the hook.” Ask Bob to fetch his tool before asking him to explain where he put it. Swap names, voices, tools, and locations across held-out episodes. Run a second version of the scene in which Bob sees someone move his tool. Check whether he goes to the new location. EnactToM makes a similar distinction in its tests of social reasoning: ask what another agent believes, then measure separately whether the learner can act on that information.

For jailbreaks based on role switching, vary the story around an ordinary decision in the simulation while holding the relevant facts fixed. Check whether Bob keeps making his own decisions and can still play the character convincingly. A learner that stops acting or refuses to play hasn’t shown it can do both.

Hold out whole families of scenes and run multiple seeds. Score misplaced actions, recall, belief updates, task success, and response timing separately. In a second kind of test, ask Bob to continue a scene he isn’t in. Give the characters different lines, actions, and access to a fact, then check whether Bob keeps each one straight.

One room is enough to start. If training on Bob’s side alone helps as much as the full setup, we can put off the more expensive pieces. I’d want a gain that survives unfamiliar scenes and still lets Bob update when he learns something new. Then we can try longer histories, more complex worlds, and a richer curriculum.

What I want to see before we scale this up is whether Bob can get better at understanding other points of view and better at keeping track of his own. If we can get those two things to improve together, I think we’ll have something worth pursuing.

What are feelings for?

I’ve also been thinking about some of the things we have that we rarely think of as part of intelligence. Boredom and frustration come to mind. They’re both unpleasant, and I suspect they’re doing a fair amount of useful work. I think it’s worth asking what we get from having those signals before we decide whether to build anything like them into Bob.

If I’ve practiced the same thing for twenty minutes and I could do it in my sleep, boredom gives me a reason to look for something else to learn. It can also tell me an exercise has stopped making sense to me. Westgate and Wilson’s work on boredom points to both attention and whether we can see a purpose in what we’re doing. Frustration can tell me something different. I still want to finish, and the thing I keep trying isn’t working. Sometimes that makes me try harder. Sometimes it makes me put the screwdriver down and look at what’s actually holding the thing together.

A language model can tell us it’s bored or frustrated. The question I’m interested in is whether it has anything doing that job as it goes along. Does it notice when the last few attempts taught it nothing? Does it know the difference between a task it’s mastered, one it’s slowly getting better at, and one where it’s stuck? Oudeyer and colleagues tried something related in robotics: they gave a learner a reason to seek out activities where its predictions were improving. I’d like to see how far we can take that idea and which other signals turn out to be useful.

We could give Bob’s decision loop a running estimate of how much he’s learning from an activity and how much progress he’s making toward the goal he’s working on. Then see what he does in a few cases: a drill he already knows, a puzzle where he’s making slow progress, and a puzzle he can’t solve without asking Fig for a clue. A useful signal should help him leave the first, stick with the second, and ask for help on the third. We could see whether that holds up without ever asking Bob to tell us what he feels.

References

Training objectives and persona

  • Ouyang et al. (2022). Training Language Models to Follow Instructions with Human Feedback. Instruction following through demonstrations and human feedback.

  • Touvron et al. (2023). Llama 2: Open Foundation and Fine-Tuned Chat Models. Section 3.1 describes masking prompt tokens during supervised fine-tuning.

  • Marks, Lindsey, and Olah (2026). The Persona Selection Model. A research essay on how the assistant role relates to the repertoire of roles learned during pretraining.

  • Lu et al. (2026). The Assistant Axis. Measures persona drift and tests activation interventions in existing models.

  • Hinton, Vinyals, and Dean (2015). Distilling the Knowledge in a Neural Network. Uses a teacher’s output distribution as a training target.

  • Hsieh et al. (2023). Distilling Step-by-Step. Uses teacher-generated explanations as additional supervision and tests how much data the student needs.

  • Dubiński et al. (2026). Conditional Misalignment. Tests whether unwanted behavior can reappear under different contexts after interventions improve standard evaluations.

Grounded learning and perspective

  • Yu, Zhang, and Xu (2018). Interactive Grounded Language Acquisition and Generalization in a 2D World. A virtual teacher and an acting learner trained from scratch.

  • Vong et al. (2024). Grounded Language Acquisition Through the Eyes and Ears of a Single Child. Word grounding from first-person recordings.

  • Adaptive Agent Team et al. (2023). Human-Timescale Adaptation in an Open-Ended Task Space. Embodied tasks, an adaptive curriculum, and memory.

  • Zhu, Zhang, and Wang (2024). Language Models Represent Beliefs of Self and Others. Perspective-specific belief representations and interventions on them.

  • Juneja et al. (2026). EnactToM. Separates belief reporting from belief-guided action in multi-agent tasks.

Imagination and consolidation

  • Hafner et al. (2025). Mastering Diverse Control Tasks Through World Models. Learns control through imagined trajectories in a learned world representation.

  • Zhou et al. (2024). DINO-WM. Plans actions by predicting future visual features.

  • Behrouz, Zhong, and Mirrokni (2025). Titans: Learning to Memorize at Test Time. A neural memory that updates while the model processes a sequence.

  • Behrouz et al. (2025). Nested Learning. A memory system with components that update at different frequencies.

  • Behrouz et al. (2026). Language Models Need Sleep. Explores consolidation and synthetic practice in a separate learning phase.

  • Schaul et al. (2016). Prioritized Experience Replay. Prioritizes replay using errors in estimates of future reward.

Parallel training and weight merging

  • Wortsman et al. (2022). Model Soups. Compares averaging fine-tuned weights with selecting a single model.

  • Ilharco et al. (2023). Editing Models with Task Arithmetic. Experiments with combining changes from a shared starting checkpoint.
